What ashwagandha is and how standardized extracts differ

Ashwagandha is an adaptogenic herb from the root of Withania somnifera, used in Ayurvedic medicine for centuries. Its bioactive compounds, called withanolides, are responsible for most of its physiological effects. The concentration and profile of these withanolides vary dramatically depending on how the root is extracted and processed, which is why not all ashwagandha supplements behave the same way in the body.

Two proprietary extracts dominate the clinical literature:

KSM-66 is a full-spectrum root extract standardized to at least 5% withanolides, produced through a water-based extraction process that preserves the natural ratio of compounds found in the whole root.

Sensoril is extracted from both roots and leaves and standardized to a higher withanolide concentration (typically 10%), with a specific focus on withaferin A.

KSM-66 has been studied primarily for stress reduction, endurance, testosterone support in men, and cognitive performance.

Sensoril has been tested more often in studies targeting metabolic outcomes, sleep quality, and body composition.

The distinction matters because the withanolide profile influences which pathways are activated. KSM-66's full-spectrum approach may offer broader adaptogenic effects, while Sensoril's higher withaferin A content may produce more pronounced effects on inflammation and metabolic signaling. Generic ashwagandha powders or capsules that don't specify standardization or extraction method are unlikely to deliver the same results seen in clinical trials, because withanolide content can vary from less than 1% to over 10% depending on growing conditions, harvest timing, and processing.

What the clinical trials show on stress, cortisol, and sleep

The evidence for ashwagandha's effects on stress and cortisol is stronger than for most herbal adaptogens, but it's not uniform across all populations or outcomes. A 2019 randomized controlled trial using 600 mg of KSM-66 daily for eight weeks found significant reductions in perceived stress scores and morning cortisol levels compared to placebo in adults with chronic stress. The cortisol reduction was modest but consistent, averaging around 25% from baseline. Importantly, the effect was most pronounced in individuals who started with elevated cortisol, not in those with normal baseline levels.

For sleep, the evidence is more mixed. A 2020 study using 600 mg of ashwagandha root extract daily for 12 weeks showed improvements in sleep quality, sleep onset latency, and total sleep time in adults with insomnia, but the effect size was moderate and not all participants responded. The mechanism here isn't direct sedation but rather a reduction in nighttime cortisol and an improvement in HPA axis regulation, which indirectly supports better sleep architecture.

Studies on anxiety show similar patterns. A meta-analysis of five RCTs found that ashwagandha significantly reduced anxiety scores compared to placebo, but the trials were small, heterogeneous in dosing, and often conducted in populations with subclinical anxiety rather than diagnosed anxiety disorders. The anxiolytic effect is mechanistically plausible through NMDA receptor modulation and GABAergic activity.

For cognitive performance, the data is thinner. A few small trials using KSM-66 or magnesium L-threonate combined with ashwagandha have shown improvements in memory and reaction time, but these findings need replication in larger, well-controlled studies. The cognitive effects are likely secondary to stress reduction rather than direct nootropic action. One critical limitation across the literature is that most trials are industry-funded, short-term (8 to 12 weeks), and conducted in relatively healthy adults.

How ashwagandha modulates the HPA axis and cortisol rhythm

Ashwagandha doesn't just lower cortisol; it modulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, the body's central stress response system. Under chronic stress, the HPA axis becomes hyperreactive, leading to elevated morning cortisol, blunted cortisol awakening response, and disrupted diurnal rhythm. Withanolides appear to dampen this overactivation through several pathways.

First, they act on GABA-A receptors in the brain, enhancing inhibitory neurotransmission and reducing excitatory tone. This is similar to how benzodiazepines work, but with far weaker affinity and no risk of dependence. Second, withanolides influence glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity, which affects how cells respond to cortisol signaling. When the HPA axis is chronically activated, glucocorticoid receptors can become desensitized, leading to a state where cortisol is elevated but less effective. Ashwagandha may help restore receptor sensitivity, allowing the body to respond appropriately to stress without overproducing cortisol.

Third, ashwagandha affects the balance between cortisol and DHEA-S, an adrenal androgen that counteracts some of cortisol's catabolic effects. Studies show that ashwagandha supplementation can reduce the cortisol-to-DHEA-S ratio, which is associated with improved stress resilience and recovery (2019 rct).

The timing of these effects matters. Cortisol follows a natural diurnal rhythm, peaking within 30 minutes of waking and declining throughout the day to reach its lowest point at night. Chronic stress flattens this curve, leading to elevated evening cortisol and disrupted sleep. Ashwagandha's effect on cortisol rhythm is most evident in the morning (where it reduces the exaggerated cortisol spike seen in stressed individuals) and in the evening (where it helps restore the natural decline).

How much to take, which form, and when

Form

If you're choosing between KSM-66 and Sensoril, the decision should be guided by your primary goal. For stress reduction, cognitive performance, and testosterone support in men, KSM-66 at 300 to 600 mg daily is the most studied option (2025 meta-analysis). For sleep quality, metabolic support, and body composition, Sensoril at 125 to 250 mg daily has more targeted evidence (2021 literature review). Both are effective; the difference is in the withanolide profile and the outcomes they've been tested for. Generic ashwagandha powders or non-standardized capsules are not interchangeable with these extracts and should not be expected to produce the same results.

Dose

The clinical dose range for stress reduction is 300 to 600 mg of a standardized extract daily, typically divided into two doses. Most studies use 300 mg twice daily or 600 mg once daily (2024 meta-analysis). Higher doses have been tested for safety without showing additional benefit for stress outcomes, though they may be relevant for other applicationsic support. A gradual approach, starting at a lower dose and adjusting based on response, is commonly recommended. The upper tolerable limit isn't well defined, but doses above 1,000 mg daily have not been studied long-term (2023 systematic review).

Who responds best and who should exercise caution

Ashwagandha's effects are most pronounced in individuals with elevated baseline cortisol, chronic stress, or subclinical anxiety. If your cortisol is already in the normal range and you're not experiencing significant stress, the benefit is likely to be minimal. This is a repletion effect, not a pharmacological one. The supplement is correcting a dysregulated HPA axis, not enhancing a system that's already functioning well.

Men with low testosterone may see modest improvements, particularly if low testosterone is stress-related rather than primary hypogonadism. A 2019 study in overweight men using 600 mg of KSM-66 daily for eight weeks showed a 15% increase in testosterone, but this was in the context of weight loss and improved body composition. Women with PCOS or elevated androgens should use caution, as ashwagandha can increase testosterone and DHEA-S, potentially worsening symptoms.

Older adults may benefit from ashwagandha's effects on muscle strength and recovery, but they should also be monitored for interactions with medications, particularly those affecting thyroid function, blood pressure, or blood sugar. Ashwagandha can lower blood glucose and blood pressure, which may require dose adjustments in medications for diabetes or hypertension.

Specific populations should avoid or use extreme caution with ashwagandha:

Pregnant and breastfeeding women should avoid ashwagandha, as there is insufficient safety data and some animal studies suggest potential effects on fetal development (2025 systematic review).

Individuals with autoimmune thyroid conditions (particularly Hashimoto's thyroiditis) should use caution, as ashwagandha can increase thyroid hormone production and may exacerbate autoimmune activity in some cases.

Those with Graves' disease or hyperthyroidism should not take ashwagandha.

Liver function should be monitored during prolonged use, as there have been case reports of elevated liver enzymes and hepatotoxicity associated with ashwagandha supplementation.

If you're taking medications metabolized by CYP3A4 enzymes, ashwagandha may alter drug levels, and medical supervision is warranted.

Cycling, tolerance, and whether you need to take breaks

The question of whether to cycle ashwagandha is not settled by the clinical literature, because most studies run for 8 to 12 weeks and don't assess what happens with continuous use beyond that. The rationale for cycling is based on the concern that the body may adapt to the HPA axis modulation, reducing the supplement's effectiveness over time. This is plausible but not proven.

Some practitioners recommend a 2-weeks-on, 1-week-off cycle, or a 3-months-on, 1-month-off cycle, to prevent tolerance. The logic is that taking periodic breaks allows the HPA axis to reset and prevents downregulation of the receptors ashwagandha acts on. However, there's no direct evidence that tolerance develops with continuous use, and some users report sustained benefits for six months or longer without cycling.

If you've been taking ashwagandha for several months and notice diminishing effects, a break of two to four weeks is reasonable to assess whether the benefit returns. If it does, that suggests some degree of tolerance had developed. If it doesn't, the initial benefit may have been placebo or the underlying stressor may have resolved.

Another reason to cycle is to reassess whether you still need it. Ashwagandha is not a long-term solution to chronic stress; it's a tool to support the body while you address the root causes. If you've been taking it for three months and your stress levels haven't improved, the issue may not be cortisol dysregulation but something else entirely (like sleep deprivation, poor nutrition, or unresolved psychological stressors).

Side effects, interactions, and what to watch for

Ashwagandha is well tolerated in most clinical trials, but side effects do occur:

The most common are gastrointestinal (including nausea, diarrhea, and stomach upset), particularly at higher doses or when taken on an empty stomach.

Drowsiness is another reported side effect, particularly when taken during the day, and is more common with evening dosing but can occur in sensitive individuals at any time.

Thyroid function changes are a more serious concern, as ashwagandha can increase T3 and T4 levels (beneficial in subclinical hypothyroidism but problematic in individuals with normal or elevated thyroid function).

Liver enzyme elevation has been reported in a small number of cases, some of which progressed to clinical hepatotoxicity.

If you're on thyroid medication, monitor TSH, free T3, and free T4 regularly, as dose adjustments may be needed. If you have hyperthyroidism or Graves' disease, do not take ashwagandha. Monitor liver function if you're taking ashwagandha long-term, particularly at doses above 600 mg daily or in combination with other supplements or medications that stress the liver (2025 rct).

Ashwagandha may interact with immunosuppressants, as it has mild immune-stimulating effects. If you're on medications for autoimmune conditions or post-transplant, consult your physician before starting ashwagandha. It may also potentiate the effects of sedatives, benzodiazepines, and other CNS depressants.

Testing your cortisol status and tracking whether it's working

Symptom relief is one measure of whether ashwagandha is working, but it's incomplete. Cortisol dysregulation can be present even when you don't feel overtly stressed, and subjective stress scores don't always correlate with objective HPA axis function. Testing cortisol before and during supplementation gives you a more accurate read on whether the intervention is addressing a real physiological issue.

Serum cortisol is the most accessible marker, but it's a snapshot of a single moment in time and doesn't capture the diurnal rhythm. Morning cortisol (measured within 30 minutes of waking) is the most informative single time point, as it reflects the cortisol awakening response and is often elevated in chronic stress. A four-point salivary cortisol test (measuring cortisol at waking, midday, evening, and bedtime) provides a fuller picture of the diurnal curve and is more sensitive to the flattening or inversion seen in HPA axis dysfunction.

DHEA-S is another useful marker, as it reflects adrenal reserve and counterbalances cortisol's effects. A high cortisol-to-DHEA-S ratio suggests chronic stress and adrenal overactivation, and ashwagandha has been shown to reduce this ratio in clinical trials (2019 rct). Tracking both cortisol and DHEA-S together gives you a more complete picture than cortisol alone.

Additional markers to monitor include:

Thyroid function (TSH, free T3, and free T4) should be checked at baseline and again after three months of use, particularly if you have a history of thyroid issues or are on thyroid medication.

Inflammatory markers like hsCRP can provide context for ashwagandha's broader effects, as chronic stress is often accompanied by low-grade inflammation.

Functional indicators include sleep quality (measured through wearable devices or sleep diaries) and heart rate variability, which reflects autonomic nervous system balance and is often impaired in chronic stress.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between KSM-66 and Sensoril ashwagandha extracts?

KSM-66 is a full-spectrum root-only extract standardized to at least 5% withanolides using a water-based process, most studied for stress reduction, testosterone support in men, and cognitive performance at 300 to 600 mg daily. Sensoril is extracted from both roots and leaves, standardized to 10% withanolides with a higher withaferin A concentration, and is more often used in studies targeting sleep quality, metabolic outcomes, and body composition at 125 to 250 mg daily. They are not interchangeable because their withanolide profiles activate different pathways.

Should I take ashwagandha in the morning or at night for stress relief?

The best timing depends on your cortisol pattern and primary symptom. Morning dosing makes mechanistic sense if your main issue is elevated waking cortisol, morning anxiety, or daytime stress management. Evening dosing is more appropriate for difficulty winding down, elevated nighttime cortisol, or poor sleep. Split dosing—300 mg morning and 300 mg before bed—addresses both ends of the cortisol diurnal curve and is supported by the most common trial design for stress reduction.

Is it safe to take ashwagandha every day long-term without cycling?

Most clinical trials run only 8 to 12 weeks, leaving the safety of continuous long-term use inadequately studied. Case reports have documented reversible adrenal suppression and elevated liver enzymes in individuals using ashwagandha chronically. A common practical approach is 3 months on followed by a 1-month break, which allows the HPA axis to reset and provides an opportunity to reassess whether benefits are continuing. Thyroid and liver function monitoring is advisable during extended use.

What are the most common side effects of ashwagandha and how can I minimize them?

The most common side effects are gastrointestinal—nausea, diarrhea, and stomach upset—especially at higher doses or when taken on an empty stomach. Taking ashwagandha with food reduces GI symptoms in most users. Drowsiness can occur, particularly with evening dosing. Thyroid function changes are a more serious concern and may go unnoticed without testing. Starting at a lower dose (300 mg) and titrating up based on response is a reasonable approach to minimizing side effects.

How do I know if ashwagandha is actually working for me?

Symptom relief alone is an incomplete measure. Testing morning cortisol and DHEA-S before starting supplementation and again after 8 to 12 weeks provides an objective baseline. If your cortisol decreases and your cortisol-to-DHEA-S ratio improves, ashwagandha is producing the physiological changes associated with benefit. If cortisol remains unchanged but subjective stress improves, placebo effects or unrelated lifestyle factors may account for the perceived benefit. Sleep quality and heart rate variability tracked via wearables can provide additional objective data.

Can ashwagandha be taken with other supplements or medications?

Ashwagandha combines safely with magnesium and most standard supplements. However, it should not be taken with benzodiazepines, barbiturates, alcohol, or other GABAergic compounds without medical supervision, as additive sedation can occur. Those on thyroid medications must monitor TSH and free T3 and T4 closely, as ashwagandha can increase thyroid hormone output and alter required medication doses. Immunosuppressant users and people on CYP3A4-metabolized medications should consult a physician before starting ashwagandha.

Does the form of ashwagandha (powder vs. extract) matter for effectiveness?

Form matters significantly. Generic ashwagandha powders or capsules without specified standardization can contain withanolide concentrations ranging from less than 1% to over 10%, making dose and effect highly unpredictable. Clinical trials almost exclusively use standardized root extracts (KSM-66 or Sensoril), not raw powders. To replicate the outcomes seen in research, look for products that specify the extract used and the withanolide percentage. Non-standardized products are unlikely to deliver consistent results.

What biomarkers should I track before and after starting ashwagandha?

The most informative markers are morning serum cortisol and DHEA-S (both reflecting HPA axis function and adrenal reserve), TSH and free T3 and T4 (since ashwagandha can alter thyroid hormone levels), and hs-CRP (systemic inflammation that ashwagandha may reduce). Retesting cortisol and DHEA-S after 8 to 12 weeks confirms whether the cortisol-to-DHEA-S ratio has improved, which is a more sensitive indicator of stress biology normalization than cortisol alone. Liver enzymes (ALT, AST) should be checked if using ashwagandha long-term at higher doses.
