Explanation

If you've noticed a sensitive tooth after deep cleaning, you're not alone. Scaling and root planing—often called a "deep cleaning"—is a common periodontal procedure to remove plaque and tartar below the gumline. While it's highly effective at preventing gum disease, it can leave your teeth feeling more sensitive than usual. This article explains why sensitivity happens, how long it lasts, and what you can do to find relief.

What Is Scaling and Root Planing?

Scaling and root planing is more thorough than a routine dental cleaning. It involves:

Scaling: Removing hardened plaque (tartar) from above and below the gumline.

Root planing: Smoothing the tooth's root surfaces so gums can reattach.

Local anesthesia: Numbing the area to minimize discomfort during the procedure.

By cleaning deep pockets around your teeth, the treatment helps prevent or halt gum disease progression. However, uncovering areas of the tooth not normally exposed can lead to temporary sensitivity.

Why Teeth Become Sensitive After Deep Cleaning

Several factors contribute to a sensitive tooth after deep cleaning:

Exposure of DentinBeneath the enamel and cementum lies dentin, a layer full of microscopic tubules connecting to your tooth's nerve center. When tartar and inflamed gum tissue are removed, these tubules can become exposed, sending sharp sensations in response to temperature or pressure.

Gum Tissue AdjustmentScaling and root planing may cause minor trauma to the gums. As your gums heal and re-attach to the cleaned root surface, slight inflammation can make nerves more reactive.

Removal of Root CementumSometimes the cementum—the thin layer covering the tooth root—is partially removed. This exposes dentin and heightens sensitivity.

Use of Dental InstrumentsVibrations from ultrasonic scalers or hand instruments can temporarily agitate the tooth's nerve endings.

Typical Timeline for Sensitivity

Knowing what to expect can ease your mind. While everyone's recovery is different, most people experience:

First 24–48 hoursSensitivity peaks as anesthesia wears off. Mild discomfort when drinking hot or cold beverages is common.

First weekSensitivity gradually decreases. You may notice slight soreness in gums and roots.

Up to two weeksMost patients see significant improvement. Occasional twinges when biting down or consuming acidic foods can still occur.

Beyond two weeksIf sensitivity persists or worsens after three to four weeks, contact your dentist. Prolonged sensitivity could signal other issues like receding gums or a cracked tooth.

Managing Sensitivity at Home

You don't have to suffer while your mouth heals. Try these simple strategies:

Use a desensitizing toothpasteLook for ingredients like potassium nitrate or stannous fluoride. Use it twice daily—you'll often see improvement within a week.

Choose a soft-bristled brushA gentle toothbrush reduces gum irritation and protects exposed root surfaces.

Practice gentle brushing techniqueAngle the brush at 45° to the gumline and use light circular motions.

Rinse with warm saltwaterDissolve ½ teaspoon of salt in 8 ounces of warm water. Swish for 30 seconds to soothe gum inflammation.

Apply topical fluorideOver-the-counter fluoride gels or rinses can help seal dentinal tubules.

Avoid extreme temperaturesSteer clear of very hot, cold, sugary, or acidic foods and drinks for the first few days.

Eat soft foodsFoods like yogurt, mashed potatoes, and smoothies minimize pressure on the sensitive area.

When to Seek Professional Help

In most cases, sensitivity after a deep cleaning is mild and temporary. However, consider contacting your dentist if you experience:

Sharp, shooting pain lasting beyond two weeks

Intense pain when biting or chewing

Swelling, redness, or pus around the gums

Fever or general feeling of illness
