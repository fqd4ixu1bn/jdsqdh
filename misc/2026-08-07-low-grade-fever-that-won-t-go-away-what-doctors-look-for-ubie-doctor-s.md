Explanation

A low-grade fever—typically defined as a body temperature between 100.4°F (38°C) and 102.2°F (39°C)—can be frustrating when it lingers for days or even weeks. While it often signals your immune system fighting off a mild infection, a persistent low-grade fever warrants a closer look. Below, we explain common causes, what your doctor will investigate, and steps you can take to track and manage symptoms.

Common Causes of Persistent Low-Grade Fever

Infections

Viral infections: Epstein–Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, viral hepatitis

Bacterial infections: urinary tract infections, subacute bacterial endocarditis, tuberculosis

Fungal and parasitic infections: histoplasmosis, malaria

Inflammatory and Autoimmune Conditions

Rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, inflammatory bowel disease

Vasculitis (inflammation of blood vessels)

Medications and Vaccines

Drug fever (reaction to antibiotics, anticonvulsants)

Post-vaccination fevers

Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders

Hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid)

Adrenal insufficiency

Malignancies

Lymphoma, leukemia, other cancers can trigger low-grade fevers

Other Causes

Deep-seated blood clots (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism)

Chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia

Most persistent low-grade fevers fall into one of these categories. Your doctor's goal is to pinpoint the underlying issue so the fever can resolve.

When to Be Concerned

While a brief low-grade fever is usually harmless, seek medical advice if you experience:

Comprehensive Medical History

Onset and pattern of fever (daily, intermittent, remitting)

Recent travel, exposures (animals, sick contacts)

Medication history (including over-the-counter drugs and supplements)

Past medical conditions (autoimmune disorders, cancer)

Tracking and Managing a Low-Grade Fever at Home

Keeping a detailed fever diary can help both you and your doctor spot patterns:

Record your temperature twice daily (morning and evening)

Note any accompanying symptoms (chills, sweating, aches, cough)

Track medications taken (dose, time, and effect on temperature)

Log recent exposures (travel, new pets, sick relatives)

Self-care measures may ease discomfort while you await test results:

Stay well-hydrated: water, electrolyte solutions, herbal teas

Rest and avoid strenuous activities

Use antipyretics (acetaminophen or ibuprofen) following package instructions

Dress in light layers and use light bedding

Maintain a comfortable room temperature (68°F–72°F)

Treatment Approaches

Treatment depends entirely on the underlying cause identified:

Infections:– Viral: supportive care, antiviral medications if indicated (e.g., influenza antivirals)– Bacterial: targeted antibiotics based on culture results– Fungal or parasitic: specific antifungal or antiparasitic agents

Inflammatory and Autoimmune:– Corticosteroids or disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs)– Biologic therapies for conditions like rheumatoid arthritis

Endocrine Disorders:– Antithyroid medications or radioactive iodine for hyperthyroidism– Hormone replacement if adrenal insufficiency is confirmed

Drug Fever:– Identify and discontinue the offending medication– Choose alternative therapies if needed

Malignancy:– Referral to oncology for chemotherapy, radiation, or surgery

Timely diagnosis ensures you receive the most effective treatment and helps prevent complications.

Reducing Anxiety, Staying Informed

It's normal to feel uneasy when a fever won't go away. However:

Remember that most low-grade fevers resolve once the root cause is treated.

Early evaluation and simple diagnostic tests often yield answers quickly.

Staying organized with a fever diary and symptom log empowers you and your healthcare team.
