# How Bravo's Jesse Solomon Discovered His Testicular Cancer at 24

In August 2017, Summer House star Jesse Solomon was diagnosed with testicular cancer at 24 years old. Though testicular cancer is most common among men 20 to 40 years old, the diagnosis still came as a shock to the Chicago native. Below, in his own words, Solomon shares with Men's Health when he knew something was wrong, the scary diagnosis that followed a year later, and how he's remained so mentally tough throughout it all.

AT FIRST I felt a twinge of pain, but that's a tricky thing because generally if you press on your nuts, they hurt. So I thought, yeah, it's probably nothing. But I still wanted to get a physical and an STD check. I go to the doctor and he says, "This is probably nothing, but go pee in a cup and get an ultrasound and we'll know for sure."

It's the Thursday before Labor Day weekend in August of 2017. I was traveling for work and I came back and I was like, "Oh, fuck, I should probably get that ultrasound done." I go and get an STD panel and the ultrasound and all the tests. The doctor called me immediately and said, "You got to come back." I went to work the whole day and thought I had an STD. I come back at 6:30 p.m. after work, and the only person left was some schmuck doctor because it was the Thursday of a holiday weekend. He was like, "Hey, so listen, you have cancer. We don't know for sure if you have cancer, but you're going to have to get your testicle removed and then we'll know for sure."

Naturally, I went to Dewey Beach with my friends that weekend because I was like, "I'm not going to sit and be sad in my apartment over Labor Day weekend." Then I came back, went to Memorial Sloan Kettering, and got a bunch more tests done. They said, "Yeah, you got to get this out. We've got to schedule this quickly." So my family flew into New York from Chicago and we got it done right away. It was a super painful surgery, kinda like somebody cuts a hole in your stomach and rips one of your nuts out because that's what happened. Then they tested it and they're like, "Yeah, we got it done quick enough. You don't need chemo right now, but we're just going to keep an eye on it."

I WAS FINE for a year. I would get checkups every six weeks and they would do blood tests for tumor markers and chest x-rays and occasionally a CT scan. Typically, the nurse practitioner walks into the office and says, "Yep, you're all good to go." This time, the doctor who did my surgery showed up in the office and I was like, "Oh shit, it's going down." My blood markers came back elevated. He was like, "Yeah, you're probably going to need either another surgery or radiation or chemo or a combination."

I said on Summer House that it spread to my stomach and that wasn't technically correct. It spread to the lymph nodes near my stomach, which is the natural next place that it would go. If stage one is getting your testicle removed, I had stage two where it spreads into the lymph nodes. They were like, "Listen, you have a great prognosis. If we do these two drugs and chemo, the survival rate's pretty high. You're young and you'll bounce back." I thought, Sloan Kettering, they're the best in the country. Just trust them. I didn't know anything about anything. Nobody in my family has a history of cancer, so this was all new for me.

Starting in July 2018, I did four rounds of five days in a row where I would wake up super early and Uber to the Upper East Side with a member of my family (my mom took two of the weeks, my dad took a week, and my brother and sister-in-law shared a week), and hang around the waiting room. You see all these other kids that are also bald—some people have it way worse than you, and some people are close to done. You make friends and you sit there with the thing in your arm for hours and hours. I played a lot of guitar. I listened to podcasts. I would read, but you can only do so much of that stuff before it's just boring. I would look at what meal I was going to eat next. Then you go home, take a nap, smoke some weed maybe, eat a meal, and wake up and do it again.

During chemo, I was fully bald. By the end of it, I was losing my eyebrows, but that part you're prepared for. It sucks because I was self-conscious about being bald, but I ended up looking solid as a bald guy. I actually have a fine-shaped head, which I was pretty surprised by. But yeah, I mean, you feel sick all the time. I got pneumonia twice and I got the flu. I wasn't throwing up or anything from the chemo, but getting pneumonia while you're in chemo is miserable. I was working out for the first half of it, and then it started to get worse and worse. Going into the second round, I had a cold and they had to delay my chemo. Then I got this shot after every week of chemo to keep my white blood cells high enough to be able to go into chemo the following week.

Then I had a cold the third week, but my white blood cells were still good enough, so I went into the chemo anyways and I got the flu. So there's just all sorts of terrible shit all the time. Every time you got a fever above 100 or something, I had to go to the emergency room where everybody also has cancer and you don't get any special treatment. You're waiting around forever overnight and you feel like shit. The whole thing was not a ton of fun. I finished chemo in October of 2018. It took four months to get through the four rounds, even though it was supposed to be every third week.

Throughout the process, I was pretty confident that I would be okay because the prognosis was good. You just gotta put on a smile and grit your teeth and bear it. There's always that little voice in the back of your head that's like, what if you're the one percent or whatever the number is that's unlucky. You're really hoping that you're not fucked. I often felt like I was trying to console other people because I didn't want my family and friends to worry.

I STARTED TO feel like myself again by the end of March 2019. I don't experience any effects from the chemo today, but I did for maybe a year or two after. There was a little bit of tingling in my fingers and toes, which is a common side effect. There were also spots in my vision and ringing in my ears, but that's all kind of gone away now. I'll definitely still get weird things where in eating certain foods I'll taste the chemo or certain things that trigger memories. Going to the Upper East Side, I'll be like, "Ooh, hate it here."

Mentally, I don't think this is the best way to do it, but I try not to think about [the thought of it returning]. It's a nerve-wracking thing that's always looming in the back of your head, but you just have to be confident. The first time around, they said there was a 14 percent chance of a recurrence before the chemo, and then after the chemo there's a three to five percent chance. The chemo really knocks it out. Every year out that you make it, you get more and more secure that it's behind you. But of course, the fact that it did come back, you have PTSD of it coming back again. Leading up to my five-year mark [last August], I was definitely getting uneasy about it, which you'll see in the show.

I have friends all the time who say, "Yeah, I feel something. I'm super nervous about it, so I'm just not going to go to the doctor." That's obviously not what you're supposed to do. Screen yourself and get a physical every year. Oftentimes, once people are 18 and their mom doesn't make them go to the doctor, they just stop going. You should go and get blood work every year.

I was pretty into working out before cancer, but it was mostly just to look good. After cancer, I'm way more diligent about looking good and feeling good, but I take into account nutrition way more. I try to do things for cancer prevention, like intermittent fasting, sauna, and cold plunge. I don't think the cold plunge helps with cancer, but being in the sauna is supposed to be good for you. I work out five, six days a week. I try not to eat seed oils and things that I've heard can be bad for you. Now I more so think about my insides than what I look like on the outside. I'll mix in some cardio depending on what my goals are at the time, but I pretty much lift weights and play sports.

Obviously, I wish that I didn't have this negative impact on my body, but mentally it's been such a freeing thing. I used to have FOMO when my friends would go out and stuff and I was like, "Ah, should I go out or not?" Now I'm like, "I literally don't give a shit. I'm alive and I'm healthy." It's made me feel way better about the smaller things that don't matter as much. Your health is the most important thing. I used to be so success-oriented and driven to make a ton of money and that stuff still matters to me, but it's taken a backseat compared to just being healthy.

I am very fortunate and blessed. My parents love each other, I have a brother, and we have a great family. I've been very privileged to go to a great college and nothing catastrophic had ever happened in my life. You expect that things will continue to go that way, but something that makes you realize that this could all change in a heartbeat makes you appreciate all of the amazing things that you do have in your life. It shows you who cares about you and the importance of family and which of your friends really love you and would do things for you, and which friends are just social friends that only want to hang out with you when you're partying.

Even the first time around with the surgery, they cut a hole in my stomach and I'm walking around Manhattan going to work a week later and I could barely stand up straight. I was like, damn, if somebody tried to steal my wallet right now, I'd be so fucked. I was thinking, you never know what anybody else is going through just by looking at them because I looked pretty normal with a shirt on. But all sorts of people have all sorts of things going on at home that nobody takes into account when you honk at them when you're driving down the street or something. I try to be as nice as I can to everybody in every situation.

I appreciate life and my family and friends, and I hope that everybody else can find that gratitude and appreciation without having to go through something like this. I try to live every day like that.
