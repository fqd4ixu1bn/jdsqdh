DANIEL TUKEY, 39, was fly-fishing about a mile off Cape Cod about three years ago when his phone rang. It was Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York City calling with news he’d been waiting for. Tukey, who had early stage III colorectal cancer, was getting a chance to join a clinical trial and access a new treatment to help his immune system attack his tumor.

Cancer was already a recurring theme in Tukey’s life story. In 2014, his older brother died just two weeks after a cancer diagnosis. His mother then developed a rare cancer and recovered.

After cancer struck his family, Tukey underwent genetic testing, which revealed he has Lynch syndrome, an inherited condition that makes certain cancers more likely. So he started getting cancer screenings years earlier than most guys do.

During a colonoscopy, Tukey’s doctors spotted something that follow-up PET scans revealed to be a tumor. He and his doctors discussed the typical treatments—surgery to remove the tumor and all or part of the colon, chemotherapy and radiation to destroy the tumor, or in some cases, all of the above.

Each can have serious side effects. Tukey, a single guy who works as an energy efficiency strategist, wasn’t keen on the idea of an ostomy bag, which collects waste outside your body when your colon is gone. “I was very worried that it would take away from my independence, from my ability to explore and date, and therefore really diminish my quality of life and my will to live,” he says.

[New Immunotherapy Cancer Drug](https://mendstride.snaketongueofficial.com/)

He wanted to explore every other possibility, so he got some details on his tumor: It had a genetic makeup known as mismatch repair deficient (MMRd) and microsatellite instability (MSI). At Memorial Sloan Kettering, a clinical trial was looking at a drug for tumors with just that signature.

“When you have this molecular signature, the immune system is primed to attack this tumor,” says Luis Diaz Jr., MD, head of the division of solid tumor oncology in Memorial Sloan Kettering’s department of medicine. “To defend themselves, tumors cloak themselves with a molecule called PD-1.” That makes them invisible to the immune system. An immunotherapy drug used in this trial, dostarlimab, interferes with PD-1, making tumors visible so the immune system can clear them, Dr. Diaz explains. Decades of foundational, taxpayer-funded research on the genetics of cancer paved the way for the discovery of anti PD-1 therapies, and how they might help people with MMRd or MSI. Now this drug was ready to be put to the test.

Tukey received a 30-minute IV infusion of the drug once every three weeks, and bunked with an old college roommate in New York City during treatment. “I could do everything that I was able to do before,” he says, which is quite a bit. He’s a guy who has lived alone in national parks and once spent a year traveling the country. “Maybe there was some psychological weight of what I went through, or times where I might have been dehydrated or tired, but those are really insignificant side effects when you’re going through something like cancer.”

After just his second infusion, imaging showed that his tumor was nearly undetectable. His results weren’t unique. In the small 42-patient trial, 100 percent of the subjects cleared their tumors and didn’t need traditional treatments.

Dr. Diaz suspects that the trial participants will live just as long as, or even longer than, people who’ve never had cancer. Tukey continued treatment for nine months and now sees his doctors every six months. “People get ‘scanxiety,’ anxiety before a scan, but I don’t really have that feeling anymore,” he says. “Part of the beauty of this is if it returns, I can go right back on that treatment, and it should be just as effective as it was the first time—something that doesn’t really happen in the world of cancer, outside of immunotherapy in this trial.”
